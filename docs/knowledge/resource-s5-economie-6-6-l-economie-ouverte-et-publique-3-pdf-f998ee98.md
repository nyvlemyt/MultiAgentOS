# S5 - Economie — 6 - 6__L_économie_ouverte_et_publique-3.pdf

## Thesis

In an open economy, international trade, exchange rates, and state intervention are three interdependent forces shaping national income — unified by the extended GDP identity Y = C + I + G + (X − M) and the twin-deficit identity (X − M) = (S − I) + (T − G).

## Context

Macroeconomics lecture (Efrei, V. Giust, Nov. 2024) extending the standard closed-economy framework to incorporate trade flows, currency markets, and fiscal policy. Draws on Ricardo's comparative advantage (Principles of Political Economy, 1817), Mundell's impossibility trilemma, and Musgrave's three functions of economic policy (1989). Illustrated with data from the US, China, UK, Singapore, and OECD countries.

## Reasoning

1. **Gains from trade and comparative advantage.** Countries trade because specialisation increases total wealth. Even without an absolute advantage, a country gains by specialising where its relative productivity is highest (Ricardo's example: Portugal produces wine at 66 % of English cost, cloth at 90 % — Portugal specialises in wine, England in cloth). Three root causes: unequal natural resources, unequal factor endowments (capital/labour), unequal distribution of final consumers.

2. **Globalisation and global value chains (GVCs).** Decades of integration have fragmented production: a single good (e.g. Boeing 787) is assembled from components produced across dozens of countries. The sum of exports + imports can far exceed GDP (Singapore 320 %, Vietnam 208 %, vs USA 28 %).

3. **Nominal exchange rate (TCN).** The TCN (noted e) is the price of a foreign currency in domestic units. When e rises the domestic currency appreciates (more foreign units per domestic unit); when e falls it depreciates. Central banks can peg the TCN — a price floor/ceiling on the forex market compensated by FX interventions (e.g. China pre-2008 kept the yuan artificially weak by buying dollars). Mundell's trilemma: a country can simultaneously achieve only two of the following three — fixed exchange rate / autonomous monetary policy (setting short-term interbank rates) / free capital mobility.

4. **Real exchange rate (TCR).** TCR = TCN × (Price level domestic / Price level foreign). It corrects for inflation differentials and reflects true purchasing power. If domestic inflation is higher than foreign inflation with an unchanged TCN, the real exchange rate rises (more purchasing power abroad). If the TCN depreciates by the same amount as the inflation differential, the TCR is unchanged.

5. **Open-economy GDP and the trade balance.** GDP expands to Y = C + I + (X − M). Rearranging: (X − M) = Y − C − I = S − I. A trade surplus means the country saves more than it invests domestically; its surplus savings are reinvested abroad. A trade deficit means the country invests more than it saves and must attract foreign capital. The world as a whole is a closed economy: the sum of all trade balances = 0.

6. **Savings, investment, and capital flows.** Commercial imbalances are always offset by symmetric financial flows. Foreign capital is attracted by the real return r: raising short-term rates → higher r → capital inflows → wider trade deficit possible, but also currency appreciation → asset prices rise → yields fall in foreign currency → interest-rate parity re-establishes equilibrium. A balance-of-payments crisis occurs when a deficit country can no longer attract foreign capital and is forced to compress imports. The net international investment position (NIIP) accumulates past trade history: China (persistent surplus) holds large foreign assets; the US (persistent deficit) hosts large foreign investment.

7. **State intervention and fiscal policy.** Musgrave's three functions of economic policy: (i) macroeconomic stabilisation (reduce output gap — Keynesian role); (ii) resource allocation (public goods: R&D, education, environment); (iii) redistribution (progressive taxation, social transfers). Government spending G is financed by taxes T, monetary creation (risks inflation), or borrowing (bond issuance → public debt). The annual bond issuance covers both the current deficit and the rollover of maturing debt.

8. **Complete GDP equations.** With government: Y = C + I + G + (X − M). Savings net of taxes: S = Y − C − T, so S + T = Y − C. Combining gives the twin-deficit identity: (X − M) = (S − I) + (T − G). The trade balance equals the sum of the private sector's net savings gap and the public sector's budget balance.

## Trade-offs

- **Mundell trilemma**: fixing the exchange rate forces a choice between monetary sovereignty and free capital flows — no country can have all three simultaneously.
- **Higher interest rates**: attract foreign capital and widen the financeable trade deficit, but appreciate the currency and contract domestic activity.
- **Fiscal deficit (G > T)**: can stimulate demand (Keynesian stabilisation) but worsens the twin-deficit position if private savings do not compensate, and accumulates public debt whose service absorbs future tax revenues.
- **Monetisation of debt**: eliminates refinancing risk but expands the money supply and risks inflation, ultimately eroding the real exchange rate.
- **Fixed vs floating exchange rate**: a peg makes trade pricing stable but surrenders a key business-cycle adjustment tool; a float restores monetary autonomy at the cost of exchange-rate volatility for exporters and importers.
