# S5 - Economie — Energie Prix.docx

## Thesis

Energy prices are structurally different from other prices — their extreme elasticity asymmetry, near-zero marginal cost in renewables, and inelastic demand mean small volume changes produce outsized price swings, which central banks cannot realistically offset, and which demand active government management of both volatility and the energy-economy transition.

## Context

Written August 2022 by Martin Sandbu (FT Free Lunch) during the European energy crisis triggered by Russia's reduction of gas supply following its invasion of Ukraine. European gas prices had risen ~10× their decade average; electricity prices followed. The article situates the crisis within broader structural questions about how energy markets behave and what policy can and cannot do.

## Reasoning

Four analytical threads are woven together: (1) Scale — a 1000% energy price rise cannot be offset by monetary policy without requiring catastrophic deflation everywhere else; the arithmetic makes central-bank 'blame' incoherent. (2) Supply curve shape — energy supply is flat (elastic) until capacity limits are hit, then near-vertical (inelastic); Putin exploited this by withdrawing just enough Russian gas to push Europe onto the vertical segment, multiplying price impact far beyond volume withdrawn. (3) Demand inelasticity — industrial energy users cannot rapidly substitute, so the 'marginal tail wags the aggregate dog': tiny volume reductions trigger system-wide price explosions. (4) Volatility is symmetric — prices can crash as fast as they spike (Germany may fill storage; renewables can produce negative prices in high-wind conditions); governments must be ready for both directions.

## Trade-offs

Price caps vs. targeted grants: capping prices below market-clearing levels destroys the incentive to economise and invest in efficiency, perpetuating the demand inelasticity that makes Europe vulnerable; grants preserve price signals while protecting purchasing power. Temporary vs. permanent shock: if the crisis is transient, policy should cushion and let disinflationary forces work; if structural, central banks must prevent wage-price spirals and governments must fund energy-intensity restructuring — and the two paths require incompatible instruments chosen in real time under uncertainty. Windfall taxation: heavier taxation of energy-sector windfalls is the political quid pro quo for public guarantees needed to de-risk renewables investment in a high-volatility environment.
