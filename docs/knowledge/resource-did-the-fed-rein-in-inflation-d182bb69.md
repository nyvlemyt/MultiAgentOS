# Did the Fed Rein in Inflation?

## Thesis

The US Federal Reserve deserves partial credit for taming inflation in 2022-23, but the disinflation was achieved through multiple transmission mechanisms — not the classic output-employment channel — aided critically by the unwinding of pandemic-era supply-chain disruptions.

## Context

By late 2023, US headline CPI had fallen to ~3.2% (from 6.5% in 2022), while unemployment remained below 4% and GDP growth stayed positive (~2.3% annualised) — a 'soft landing' most economists had not expected. Other developed economies (Canada, eurozone, UK) lagged the US on both growth and disinflation. The article (Jeffrey Frankel, Project Syndicate, Nov 2023) asks whether the Fed and Biden administration truly earned credit for this outcome.

## Reasoning

Three transmission mechanisms linked Fed rate hikes to lower inflation, bypassing the usual output contraction route: (1) **Housing demand** — sharply higher 30-year mortgage rates compressed housing demand. (2) **Exchange rate** — the USD appreciated >8% against major currencies from March 2022, dampening import prices. (3) **Commodity prices** — the global commodity index fell >30% in dollar terms (Mar 2022–Oct 2023), a predictable consequence of higher real interest rates reducing speculative and inventory demand. The puzzle of why disinflation came with so little job loss has two candidate explanations: (a) a steep Phillips curve near full employment — below 4% unemployment, demand compression hits prices rather than jobs; (b) the dominant factor: supply-chain normalisation. The NY Fed Global Supply Chain Pressure Index peaked Dec 2021 and fell steadily from Apr 2022. As bottlenecks dissolved, aggregate supply expanded, which by itself could have produced either lower inflation or higher output. That it showed up as lower inflation (not higher growth) suggests monetary tightening absorbed the supply dividend — preventing a re-acceleration that would have occurred had rates stayed near zero.

## Trade-offs

Credit is contested on two axes. First, **initial error**: US policymakers underestimated inflation risks in 2021, so the Fed was late to tighten — some of the inflation it later fought was partly policy-induced. Second, **counterfactual uncertainty**: had the Fed not raised rates, would supply-chain normalisation alone have been sufficient? The author argues no — continued near-zero rates would have kept the economy overheating, absorbing the supply improvement as output rather than price relief. Against that: the causal weight of supply normalisation vs. monetary tightening cannot be precisely decomposed, and the unusually steep Phillips curve near full employment may do significant independent work. The political dimension is noted but set aside: traditional political rules grant credit to incumbents for favourable outcomes regardless of causal responsibility.
